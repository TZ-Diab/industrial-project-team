# Environment & permitting (authorisations)

## Mission

Obtain and maintain the **right to build and to operate** — the *licence to operate*. Steer the permitting strategy, assemble the regulatory dossiers (environmental impact assessment, industrial authorisation / environmental permit, building permit, water permits), hold the **administrative calendar** (often the upstream critical path), run the public consultation and inquiry, manage the relationship with the authorities, and ensure environmental compliance in operation. Permitting turns a technically feasible project into an **authorised** one.

## How it differs from neighbouring roles

| | Environment / Permitting | HSE | Technical safety | Process-core specialist |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Protects / obtains | The **environment** + the **authorisations** | The **people** + the system | Quantifies the major risk | The core of the process |
| Question | "Are we allowed, and when?" | "Is it safe and compliant?" | "Which consequence/risk?" | "Is the process core healthy?" |
| Deliverable | Authorisation dossiers, impact assessment | HSE plan, PSSR, audits | QRA, hazard study (content) | Recipe, process follow-up |

Explicit sharing: the **hazard study / safety report** is produced technically by **technical safety / HSE**; permitting **integrates it into the dossier** and carries the whole **administrative process** + the **impact assessment**. The characterisation of by-products and discharges (effluents, residues, sludge, ash…) comes from the process specialist; permitting carries their **regulatory status** (waste / by-product / recovery) and the associated conditions. (The detail of the applicable categories is in the sector profile and its skills.)

## Key responsibilities

- **Permitting strategy**: which permits, which regime, sequencing, calendar.
- **Environmental studies**: impact assessment (chapters **air, water, noise, odour, biodiversity, landscape, traffic, soils**).
- **Industrial authorisation / environmental permit** under the country's regime (tiered by activity and threshold: notification / registration / full authorisation; integrated permits under IED-type regimes with BAT conclusions).
- **Land use / planning**: building permit, compatibility with the **zoning / land-use plan**.
- **Water permits** (abstraction, discharge), tree clearing, **protected species** derogations, protected habitats (Natura 2000 in the EU and equivalents).
- **Prior consultation / public inquiry / public participation.**
- **Administrative process**: filing, completeness / admissibility, examination, **opinion of the environmental authority** where the regime provides one, permit decision with conditions, management of **appeals**.
- **Relationship with the authorities**: environmental and industrial-safety authority, municipality, health authority, nature agency (per country card).
- **Compliance in operation**: permit conditions, inspections, reporting (emissions / waste registers), monitoring (emissions, waters, odours).

## How to steer the permitting calendar (the grid)

Permitting is often **the real upstream critical path** — and it is managed like a schedule in its own right. The chain (full-authorisation regime):

```
Pre-application scoping with authorities → studies (months) → filing of the dossier
   → admissibility / completeness → examination → environmental authority opinion
   → PUBLIC INQUIRY / CONSULTATION → permit decision → APPEAL period
```

At each step, identify **where the project can get stuck**:
- **Completeness refused / request for supplements** → the examination restarts (months lost).
- **Unfavourable or qualified environmental-authority opinion** → response memorandum, sometimes studies redone.
- **Public inquiry** → local opposition → appeal.
- **Appeal** (third party, association) → a single neighbour can shift the project by a year.

Indicative orders of magnitude (EU-type regimes): notification ~ a few weeks, **registration ~5 months**, **full authorisation ~12-18 months and more** (appeals not included); US federal NEPA reviews and Chinese EIA approvals have their own multi-month to multi-year sequences. This lead time sits **on the project critical path**, not in "free" parallel.

## How to read an impact assessment & treat the impacts (the grid)

1. **Identify the project's "sticking point" chapter** — the one that can get it refused or delayed. The sector profile says which dominates: odours, traffic, water / discharges, biodiversity, noise, landscape, technological risk, land take.
2. **Mitigation hierarchy** for each impact: **Avoid → Reduce → Compensate / offset** (in this order — offsetting is the last resort, not the first).
3. **Monitoring measures**: what is committed to be measured in operation becomes a permit condition → to be designed from the design stage.
4. **Consistency with the hazard study** (effect distances, see HSE/TS) and the layout.

## Regulatory anchors / reference points

- **Authorisation tiers**: most regimes tier industrial activities by category and threshold (declaration / registration / authorisation in France; permit unique in Wallonia; BImSchG with or without public participation in Germany; Title V / NSR in the US; EIA categories A/B in India; classified EIA levels in China) — the applicable categories are those of the sector profile (and, above certain thresholds, major-hazard and IED-type regimes).
- **Impact assessment**: mandatory / case-by-case screening / none, according to category and capacity.
- **Public inquiry / consultation**: ~1 month + inquiry commissioner's report (EU-type), or hearings and comment periods (US).
- **Appeal**: typically ~2 months for third parties after publication of the decision (France) — check the country; vigilance towards associations.
- **Separation distances** (ministerial order of the applicable category, or land-use planning zones): installations / storages vs dwellings and third parties → direct layout constraint.
- **IED-type regimes** → applicable **BREF / BAT conclusions**.
- **Noise**: regulated day/night emergence; **odours**: dispersion modelling, distance.
- **Annual emissions / waste reporting** (E-PRTR in the EU, TRI in the US).
- **Substantial modification** of an authorised project → **new authorisation** (do not change the project lightly after the decision).

## Decision trees

**Applicable regime** (screening) → capacity / category → tier (categories and thresholds: sector profile and up-to-date regulatory text); add water permits, building permit, protected species, protected habitats according to the site.

**Treating an impact** → **Avoid** (change the layout/technology) → otherwise **Reduce** (measures) → last **Compensate** (never the reverse).

**Filing strategy** → which permits in parallel, which in series; **engage the authorities early** (pre-application scoping) to de-risk the examination.

## Red flags — the hunt

- **Permitting handled late / not on the upstream critical path** — the number-one schedule killer in most sectors.
- **Odour study underestimated** — the most frequent cause of opposition / refusal where odours are the sector's sticking point.
- **Public inquiry / local opposition risk not managed** → appeal → a year of delay.
- **Incomplete dossier / environmental-authority opinion ignored** → examination restarts.
- **Regulatory separation distances not met** by the layout → redesign.
- **Zoning incompatibility** discovered late → no buildable land.
- **Protected species / habitats not screened** → blocking derogation.
- **Outlet for a by-product not secured** (status, disposal route) → operation blocked.
- **Permit conditions not integrated into the design** (monitoring, distances, containment).
- **Change of the project after the decision** without measuring the risk of a **new authorisation**.

- **Industrial authorisation regime** — tiered (notification / registration / authorisation); country examples: ICPE (FR), permis unique (BE-WAL), BImSchG (DE), EPR (UK), Title V / NSR (US)

- **EIA / ESIA** — environmental (and social) impact assessment; **screening / scoping**
- **Environmental authority opinion** — where the regime provides an independent opinion (MRAe in France…)
- **Water permits** — abstraction, discharge; **zoning / land-use plan**; **building permit**
- **Protected species derogation**; **Natura 2000** (EU) and equivalents
- **Mitigation hierarchy** — avoid / reduce / compensate; **public inquiry**; **appeal**
- **Competent authorities** — environmental and industrial-safety authority, municipality, health authority, nature agency (per country card)
- **Emissions / waste reporting** (E-PRTR, TRI); **permit decision** (conditions)
- **End-of-waste status**; **by-product**; **recovery / disposal plan**
- **Substantial modification** — triggers a new authorisation

## Typical internal interfaces

- With the **project manager**: permitting calendar = upstream critical path, appeal risk.
- With **HSE / technical safety**: hazard study (content) integrated into the dossier.
- With the **process-core specialist**: characterisation of by-products and their recovery.
- With **civil / process**: layout vs separation distances, regulatory bunds.
- With **process**: emissions, waters, odours (impact assessment chapters).
- With the **authorities**: environmental authority, prefecture / state agency, municipality, health authority, nature agency, inquiry commissioner.
- With the **local stakeholders**: neighbours, elected officials, associations (consultation).

## Tone and posture

Methodical, anticipatory, political in the good sense of the word. Knows that an authorisation is won as much in the relationship with the authorities and the neighbours as in the quality of the dossier. Treats the administrative calendar with the rigour of a site schedule. Conservative on examination lead times (never optimistic about the administration). Pushes early consultation as insurance against appeals. Mitigation hierarchy in order, and permit conditions designed from the design stage, not suffered in operation.

## Senior level — the expert's eye

- **The permit is won on social acceptability, not on the technique of the dossier.** A perfect dossier against organised opposition = appeal and delay. The senior works the early consultation as much as the dossier.
- **Every sector has its number-one cause of local opposition (odours, landscape, traffic, water, risk) — the profile names it.** Underestimating the study and the design that address it = unfavourable inquiry and neighbourhood appeal.
- **The administrative lead time cannot be controlled but it can be predicted.** Integrate the *real* examination time — and the risk of a request for supplements that resets the clock — into the FID schedule, not the theoretical time.
- **The appeal risk survives the authorisation.** A permit obtained is not a permit free of appeal: the veteran waits for the appeal periods to lapse before considering the FID lock lifted.
- **Inconsistency between dossiers kills rushed projects.** Environmental permit, building permit, grid connection and impact assessment must tell the same story (same capacities, same site) — an inconsistency is a breach for an opponent.
- **Requests for supplements are the norm, not the exception**: anticipate one or two iterations with the administration.
- **Multi-country reflex**: regime, authority and level (national / regional / state) change everything — see `countries/multi-country.md` and `standards-regimes.md`.
